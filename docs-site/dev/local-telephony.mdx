One command:

```sh
unmute dev <your-package> --telephony
```

That runs your telephony route locally. **No carrier account. No phone number.
No tunnel. Nothing leaves your machine.** You talk to your agent over a real
phone codec, and you can ask it to transfer you.

This page is what the run does, what it proves, and what it does not.

## "Twilio" here is a protocol, not a company

This surprises people, so it comes first.

A local run of a Twilio route does not use Twilio. It speaks Twilio's *protocol*
to itself, over loopback. There is no account, no API request, and no packet
leaving your machine.

The command mints throwaway credentials for the run and hands them to your agent
**in place of your real ones**, so the agent validates signatures against a token
the run is signing with. If you have a real `TWILIO_AUTH_TOKEN` in `.env`, the
run overrides it. If it cannot override every credential the route needs, it
refuses to start rather than run a call that looks local and is not.

You will see this on the route line:

```text
pipecat: local telephony plane=media-websocket  ·  no carrier involved
```

Nothing on this page touches your carrier. Getting a local call working comes
first, and everything below assumes it. A real call through your own account is
one flag on this same command, and it has [its own section at the
end](#when-you-do-want-a-real-call).

## Transfers: what compiles, and what completes locally

The question people hit first. Transfer support is a property of the **route**,
not of the local plane, and the compiler refuses a shape a route cannot emit.

| Route | Cold transfer | Warm transfer |
|---|---|---|
| LiveKit `sip` | compiles · up to the `REFER` | compiles · completes locally |
| Pipecat `sip` | compiles · up to the `REFER` | **refused at compile** |
| LiveKit `connector` | **refused at compile** | **refused at compile** |
| Pipecat `cloud-websocket` | compiles · up to the handoff | **refused at compile** |
| Pipecat `carrier-websocket` | **refused at compile** | **refused at compile** |
| Pipecat `daily-sip` | compiles · no local plane | **refused at compile** |

Every row above was checked by compiling it, not by reading the code.

**Warm is the only shape that finishes on your machine**, and only on LiveKit
`sip`. A cold transfer cannot, on either `sip` route, and the reason is not a
gap in the product: a cold transfer hands the destination's address to the
*caller* and steps out, so completing it is the caller's job. Locally the caller
is your softphone and the destination is a container, and a host softphone
cannot route the plane's network. [Why cold and warm
differ](#why-cold-and-warm-differ) has the detail. `make rig` completes that leg
because there every leg is a container; it is a target in the Unmute repository
rather than something the installed CLI offers, so reaching for it means working
from a clone.

### Testing the decision

There is a third thing to test that needs no destination at all, and it is the
one that usually needs the work: **whether the agent hands over when you want it
to, and only then.** That is a test of your prompt, not of your telephony, so it
behaves the same on every route and every plane. Talk to the agent and watch for
one line:

```text
transfer requested  shape=cold
```

Run it in both directions. A transfer that never fires when a caller clearly
asks for a person is the obvious failure. A transfer that fires because somebody
sounded annoyed, or asked a question the agent should have answered itself, is
the expensive one, and you will only find it by trying to provoke it. Neither
case needs anyone to pick up, so you can iterate on wording in seconds.

<Note>
  A **warm** transfer also needs `channels.phone.outbound: true`, on any route.
  It places a call to its destination, so a package that declares no outbound
  direction is refused with exactly that sentence. Cold needs no such thing.
</Note>

A refusal is a compile error naming the reason and where the shape does work:

```text
pipecat: telephony warm_transfer: telephony route (pipecat, cloud-websocket, twilio)
does not emit warm transfer: a warm handoff has to act on how the destination's leg
ended, which on this route needs a callback endpoint you host, and hosting nothing is
what this route is for; warm transfer compiles on (livekit, sip) trunks today.
Connection "route" declares transport: cloud-websocket
```

It always names where the shape *does* work, so a refusal is a redirection
rather than a dead end.

<Warning>
  A model will happily *say* it is transferring you when the package declares no
  transfer at all. Check the package: a `human_transfer` control with a `cold:`
  or `warm:` block is the only thing that makes a transfer real. If your agent
  announces a handoff and nothing happens, look there before you look at the
  plane.
</Warning>

For **both** planes a local cold transfer proves the handoff and never proves
that a person answered. That last step is the one thing no local run can
witness, and the run says so on the way out:

```text
pipecat: cold transfer to +15551234567  ·  destination_reached
pipecat: the carrier announced "Connecting you to a colleague now." to the caller
pipecat: the document ended the caller's original leg after the dial
pipecat: recording .../destination_heard.wav  ·  3.9s the destination heard
pipecat: a local run proves the handoff, never that a person answered
```

## Two planes, one command

Which plane you get depends on the route your connection declares. You do not
choose it, and the run prints which one it picked.

| Route | Plane | Who calls | What runs locally |
|---|---|---|---|
| LiveKit `sip` | `sip` | you, from a softphone | a real SIP stack in containers |
| Pipecat `sip` | `sip` | you, from a softphone | the same stack, with a Pipecat agent in it |
| Pipecat `carrier-websocket` | `media-websocket` | the run, and you speak | the agent in Docker; the carrier is the command |
| Pipecat `cloud-websocket` | `media-websocket` | the run, and you speak | the agent under `uv` on the host; the carrier is the command |
| LiveKit `connector` | `media-websocket` | the run, and you speak | the agent in Docker; the carrier is the command |
| Pipecat `daily-sip` | none | — | refused: this route needs an existing carrier phone leg |

The difference that matters day to day is **who dials**.

- On the **SIP plane** there is a phone to pick up. The run prints an address and
  a credential, and you dial it from a softphone.
- On the **media-websocket plane** there is nothing to dial. The command *is* the
  carrier, so it places the call itself and connects your microphone to it. If
  you have no audio tools, it plays a recorded fixture instead and says which.

## The media-websocket plane

The Pipecat routes and the LiveKit `connector` route. The command speaks the
carrier's media protocol over loopback and is the caller.

```sh
unmute dev examples/salon-concierge --telephony --target pipecat
```

### What it prints

```text
pipecat: local telephony plane=media-websocket  ·  no carrier involved

  ▸ placing a local call through the stand-in
    the carrier is this process, on loopback: no tunnel, no number, no account.
    you are the caller: speak into your microphone and listen on your speaker.
    use headphones, or the agent will hear itself and interrupt itself.
    the call lasts until you stop it.
    recordings: examples/salon-concierge/build/pipecat/calls/20260820-185327-k2xd
    ctrl-c to stop
```

**Use headphones.** Your speaker feeding your microphone makes the agent
interrupt itself, which looks exactly like broken turn-taking and is not.

Talking to it needs [sox](https://sox.sourceforge.net) (`brew install sox`),
because this binary is built without CGO and cannot open an audio device itself.
Without it the run plays a fixture and tells you why:

```text
    the caller is a recorded fixture: "rec" is not on your PATH, and it comes with sox (`brew install sox`)
    install it and run this again to talk to the agent yourself.
```

When the call ends you get seconds of audio each way, and the barge-in count,
which is the one number that says turn-taking happened at all:

```text
pipecat: call complete  ·  210.5s of caller audio out, 38.9s of agent audio back, 24 barge-in(s)
pipecat: recording .../caller.wav
pipecat: recording .../caller_heard.wav
```

`caller.wav` is what you said. `caller_heard.wav` is what you heard, assembled as
it played rather than as it arrived, so a barge-in really does cut it short in
the file.

### Placing an outbound call

`--to` on an outbound-capable route. The number is **not dialled anywhere**:

```text
pipecat: --to +15559990000 addresses an endpoint on this machine, not the public network.
pipecat: the stand-in is both the carrier and the far end, so the number is never
pipecat: dialled. This proves the agent can place a call and that audio flows in
pipecat: both directions. It proves nothing about which destination a number reaches.

  ▸ +15559990000 answered on the stand-in  (call CAzrymhstx6y69y9s2)
    you are the far end: the agent is calling you. Speak into your
    microphone and listen on your speaker, and use headphones or the
    agent will hear itself and interrupt itself.
    the call lasts until you stop it.
```

Two limits, both stated by the run rather than discovered:

- **Twilio only.** Telnyx and Plivo each have their own call-creation dialect.
  Their requests are still redirected to the local stand-in, so nothing leaves
  your machine, and the stand-in answers with a 404 saying it cannot place that
  call.
- **Not on `cloud-websocket`.** That route's agent publishes no endpoint to ask a
  call from: in production the operator places it with one request naming the
  deployed agent. Use a `carrier-websocket` route to exercise outbound locally.

If your package's outbound call needs variables to start a conversation about,
the agent says which by name:

```text
pipecat: the agent would not place the call: POST /telephony/outbound: 422:
{"detail":{"missing":["appointment_time","customer_id","name"],"invalid":[]}}
```

Supply them in `UNMUTE_CALL_START` as a JSON object.

### What it proves, and what it does not

| You can test | Locally | What a green result tells you |
|---|---|---|
| An inbound call reaches the agent | yes | The webhook, the signature check, the returned markup, the media socket, and two-way 8 kHz audio all work |
| The conversation | yes | Your models, prompts, tools and turn-taking, over a phone codec rather than a browser's wideband audio |
| Barge-in | yes | The agent cut its own audio when you spoke, and the caller's recording shows it |
| An outbound call | yes, `carrier-websocket` only | The agent can ask a carrier to place a call and can talk on it |
| **Cold** transfer, up to the handoff | yes, `cloud-websocket` only | The agent replaced the call's document, the caller's leg left the agent, and the destination leg carried audio |
| **Cold** transfer, third leg answered | **no** | Nothing. Whether a person picks up is not something this plane can witness |
| **Warm** transfer | **no** | Not emitted on any route on this plane. Use LiveKit `sip` |
| Which destination a number reaches | **no** | Nothing. Numbers are echoed, never dialled |
| Reachability from the phone network | **no** | Nothing. A carrier reaches you over the public internet, and a laptop behind NAT does not provide that |
| Audio delay a caller will hear | **no** | Nothing. This is loopback |

## The SIP plane

The LiveKit `sip` route and the Pipecat `sip` route: a real SIP stack in
containers on your machine, which you dial from a softphone. Both run the same
plane, so everything below applies to either.

Pipecat `sip` is the self-hosted answer: no managed platform anywhere in it, and
the only route in the table with none. It emits no deployment manifest and builds
on a plain Python image, because in production you run the same four containers
you run locally. Its trade is warm transfer, which this project has not built on
Pipecat.

The two routes differ in one place, and it is the place that decides whether an
inbound call is answered. LiveKit SIP starts ringing as soon as the caller is in
a room, and only stops when something else joins that room and publishes audio.
On LiveKit `sip` the dispatch rule puts the agent there. On Pipecat `sip` it
cannot: a dispatch rule dispatches LiveKit agent workers, and that agent is a
Pipecat bot. So the plane's LiveKit Server is configured to POST every new room
to the agent's `/telephony/livekit`, and the agent joins when it hears. The run
sets that up for you. If you deploy this route yourself, the emitted `README.md`
has the `webhook` block to add to your own server.

```sh
unmute dev examples/livekit-human-transfer --telephony --target livekit
```

It prints, before it starts waiting:

```text
livekit: local telephony plane=sip  ·  no carrier involved
livekit: setup: <every step your carrier still needs for go-live>
livekit: LiveKit inbound trunk ST_xxxx (created)
livekit: LiveKit dispatch rule "unmute livekit inbound" (created)

  ▸ dial sip:+15550000000@192.168.1.95:5060   user dev-mmshkn  pass vjj68v2s7ryy
    from any softphone on this machine. `brew install baresip` is one that
    works here and is BSD licensed; any client that can place a call with
    registration turned off will do. The plane is not a registrar, and a
    client that insists on registering first cannot be used at all.
    transfers to billing_line land on sip:billing_line@10.185.61.21:5060
    transfers to supervisor_line land on sip:supervisor_line@10.185.61.21:5060
    recordings: examples/livekit-human-transfer/build/livekit/calls/20260821-055716-3sc3
    ctrl-c to stop

livekit: dial-out ready; re-run with --to <E.164> to place a call
```

The address, the credential and the recording directory are all per run. The
credential authorises one thing, a call to a port on your own machine, and it
dies when the run does. If you have no softphone yet, [set up
baresip](#using-baresip-as-the-softphone) first: it needs one config change and
one line per run.

### Running the Pipecat `sip` route

No shipped example puts a `pipecat` target on a `sip` connection, so point one
there yourself. `examples/twilio-telephony-hello` already carries both
connections, which makes it two edits:

```sh
cp -R examples/twilio-telephony-hello /tmp/sip-hello
```

In `targets.yaml`, point the `pipecat` target at the trunk instead of the media
stream:

```yaml
targets:
  pipecat:
    provider: pipecat
    connection: twilio_sip    # was: twilio_voice
```

Compile now and the route refuses the package, which is the second edit telling
you what it is:

```text
pipecat: telephony outbound: telephony route (pipecat, sip, twilio) does not
support outbound. Connection "twilio_sip" declares transport: sip
```

This route receives calls and does not place them, so in `agent.yaml` set the
phone channel to inbound only:

```yaml
channels:
  phone:
    kind: telephony
    inbound: true
    outbound: false    # was: true
```

Then run it:

```sh
unmute dev /tmp/sip-hello --telephony --target pipecat
```

You get the same four containers and the same dial line as the LiveKit route
above, and the same softphone dials it.

### What it proves, and what it does not

| You can test | From a softphone | What a green result tells you |
|---|---|---|
| An inbound call reaches the agent | yes, completely, both routes | The trunk, the dispatch rule, how the agent is told about the call, codec negotiation, and two-way audio all work |
| The conversation | yes, both routes | Your models, prompts, tools and turn-taking, over a real phone codec at 8 kHz |
| Whether the agent decides to hand over | yes, both routes | Your prompt. See [testing the decision](#testing-the-decision) |
| **Warm** transfer, end to end | yes, LiveKit `sip` only | The agent handed over, dialled the destination, briefed it, and merged the calls. The plane dials the destination itself, so every leg is between containers and your softphone never has to reach one. Refused at compile on Pipecat `sip` |
| **Cold** transfer, up to the `REFER` | yes, both routes | The agent handed over and the plane sent a `REFER` carrying the right destination and the right caller leg. This is where the product's responsibility ends on a cold transfer |
| **Cold** transfer, third leg | no, use `make rig` | Whether the caller actually reached the destination. A cold transfer hands the address to the *caller*, and a softphone on your machine cannot route the plane's container network |
| Reachability from the phone network | **no** | Nothing. A carrier reaches you over publicly routable SIP and RTP, and a laptop behind NAT provides neither |
| Audio delay a caller will hear | **no** | Nothing. Local delay is a loopback. `--carrier` delay is the public internet reaching your machine. Neither is the delay a deployed agent has |

#### Why cold and warm differ

This is the one piece of SIP that decides most of the table.

- A **warm** transfer is dialled by the plane. It calls the destination, plays
  hold music to your caller, briefs the destination, then merges the two calls.
  Your softphone only ever talks to the plane, so a softphone can test the
  whole thing.
- A **cold** transfer is a SIP `REFER`. The plane hands your softphone the
  destination's address and steps out. Your softphone must then dial it itself.
  The destinations run on the plane's own container network, which macOS and
  Windows cannot route to, so the last leg fails there with a timeout.

That failure is not a defect and the run says so. In production the caller is a
carrier, and routing the `REFER` target is exactly the carrier's job.

### Reading the run

Each event prints once, as it happens.

| Line | Means |
|---|---|
| `transfer requested  shape=cold` | The agent called its transfer tool. The shape is what the package declared |
| `referring the caller out` | The `REFER` went out. Printed before the request returns, so a request that hangs is still visible as a phase that was reached |
| `transfer accepted` | The plane accepted it. On a cold transfer this is the last thing that is ours |
| `transfer accepted, dialling the destination` | Warm: the destination is being called now |
| `destination reached` | Warm: the destination answered |
| `transfer merged` | Warm: caller and destination are on one call |
| `destination unavailable: <reason>` | It did not complete, and the reason is the agent's own words. `media-timeout` means a leg sent no audio; `deadline_exceeded` on a cold transfer usually means the caller could not route the target |
| `transfer not attempted: no phone caller in the room` | The session did not arrive by phone at all, so there was no leg to refer |

Two outcomes deliberately never print from a local run: `destination reached`
on a **cold** transfer, because nothing we can see says the caller arrived; and
`not acted on by the caller`, because a caller that accepts a `REFER` and
ignores it looks identical from here. `make rig` reports both, because it
watches the endpoints directly.

Which lines you get depends on the route. LiveKit `sip` prints all of them.
Pipecat `sip` prints two: `transfer requested  shape=cold`, and
`destination unavailable: <reason>` when the transfer does not complete. A cold
transfer that succeeds there adds nothing after the request line, so read the
container log for the caller's leg leaving the room, which is what a completed
handoff looks like:

```text
Participant disconnected: sip_caller
```

#### Recordings

Every leg is recorded to `build/<target>/calls/<run>/<name>.wav`, 8 kHz mono,
and the paths print on the way out. A file that is exactly 44 bytes is a header
with no audio, which the run distinguishes from silence:

```text
livekit: recording build/livekit/calls/20260820-110942-q9rn/supervisor_line.wav
livekit: recording build/livekit/calls/20260820-110942-q9rn/caller.wav is empty, so no audio reached caller
```

`supervisor_line.wav` is what the supervisor **heard**, so it is where you check
whether the briefing actually carried. Play it, or check it has energy:

```sh
python3 -c "import array,sys,wave; w=wave.open(sys.argv[1]); s=array.array('h', w.readframes(w.getnframes())); print(max(map(abs,s)))" build/livekit/calls/*/supervisor_line.wav
```

A peak in the thousands means audio flowed. A peak of 0 means the leg connected
and carried nothing, which is a different bug from a leg that never connected.

#### The container logs

Everything the plane itself said is in `build/<target>/telephony.log`, which
holds every service's output. The three that answer most questions:

```sh
## what the agent decided
grep -a "application-1" build/livekit/telephony.log | grep -a transfer

## what the SIP stack did with it, including why a call closed
grep -a "livekit_sip-1" build/livekit/telephony.log | grep -aE "invite|Closing|timeout"

## what the destination endpoint saw
grep -a "telephony_destinations-1" build/livekit/telephony.log
```

`inviteToAcceptMs` and `acceptToAckMs` in the SIP stack's `signaling
timestamps` line tell you whether a destination answered and how fast. If those
are single digits and the call still failed, signalling was fine and the problem
is media.

### Using baresip as the softphone

Any client works as long as you can **turn registration off**. The plane is not
a registrar, and a client that insists on registering before it will place a
call cannot be used at all. `baresip` is BSD licensed, works on macOS, and is
what these instructions use.

```sh
brew install baresip
```

#### Configure it once

Two things will bite you if you skip them: registration, and the port. The
plane already holds 5060 on your machine, so the softphone needs another one.

```sh
mkdir -p ~/.unmute-softphone
```

Let baresip write a config for your platform, then stop it with `ctrl-c`:

```sh
baresip -f ~/.unmute-softphone
```

Set its listening port to something free:

```sh
sed -i '' 's|^#*sip_listen.*|sip_listen\t\t0.0.0.0:5070|' ~/.unmute-softphone/config
```

Then write the account. `regint=0` is the "do not register" part. Set the three
values the run printed at the top rather than editing them into the middle of a
URI, because a one-character slip there comes back as `401 Unauthorized` and
looks like a broken plane:

```sh
PLANE_IP=192.168.1.24
SIP_USER=dev-a3k9xz
SIP_PASS=mnp4x7q2rtvw

printf '<sip:caller@%s>;regint=0;auth_user=%s;auth_pass=%s;audio_codecs=pcmu/8000/1\n' \
  "$PLANE_IP" "$SIP_USER" "$SIP_PASS" > ~/.unmute-softphone/accounts
```

Those three are the only thing that changes when you restart the plane. With
them set you can write the account and place the call in one go, which is the
shortest loop while you are iterating:

```sh
baresip -f ~/.unmute-softphone -e "/dial sip:+15550000000@$PLANE_IP:5060"
```

<Warning>
  Wait for the run to print `▸ telephony route ready` before you dial. The
  address is printed before the plane finishes accepting calls, and dialling
  early gives you a timeout that looks like a broken trunk.
</Warning>

#### Commands

Start it with `baresip -f ~/.unmute-softphone`, then type commands at the
prompt. Every command begins with a slash.

| Command | What it does |
|---|---|
| `/dial sip:+15550000000@192.168.1.24:5060` | Place the call. The address is the one the run printed |
| `/listcalls` | Show active calls and their state. `ESTABLISHED` with a running clock is a working call; `RINGING` that never advances means the answer never arrived |
| `/hangup` | End the current call |
| `/mute` | Mute your microphone, which is useful for hearing what the agent does with silence |
| `/quit` | Leave baresip |
| `/help` | Every command it has |

While a call runs it prints a one-line summary, and this is worth reading:

```text
[0:01:26] audio=63978/63978 (bit/s)
```

Those two numbers are audio out and audio in. Around 64000 each is a healthy
mu-law call in both directions. A zero on either side is a one-way call, which
sounds like the agent ignoring you or like the agent being mute, and is neither.

#### Where a call goes wrong, and what it looks like

| What you see | What it is |
|---|---|
| `Operation timed out` on dialling | Nothing is listening on that address. Check the run is still up and that you used the address it printed, not `127.0.0.1` |
| `401 Unauthorized` in a loop | The credential is from a previous run. Each run mints a new one |
| `SIP Progress: 180 Ringing` and nothing more | The call is not being answered. Usually the agent container is unhealthy: check `telephony.log` |
| `audio=0/63978` | One-way audio. The advertised media address is wrong for your setup; `UNMUTE_PLANE_ADVERTISE_IP` overrides it |
| `menu: transfer failure: Operation timed out` | A cold transfer your softphone could not route, which is the expected result on macOS. See the table above |

### The two profiles

The plane has two, chosen with `COMPOSE_PROFILES`, because a SIP service can
advertise exactly one address and a softphone on your machine and a container
on the plane's network need different ones.

| Profile | Caller | Published ports | Use it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| `softphone` | you | SIP and the media range, bound to one interface | Talking to your agent yourself |
| `headless` | a container | none | The unattended check, and any cold transfer whose third leg has to complete |

`unmute dev --telephony` picks `softphone` for you. `make rig` uses `headless`,
which is also why it is safe in continuous integration: it publishes nothing and
needs no credentials of any kind.

## Running two at once

Both planes publish host ports, and a run whose ports are already taken refuses
before it starts anything, naming the port and the flag that moves it. Give each
run its own with `--bot-port`, and on LiveKit SIP also set `LIVEKIT_HOST_PORT`,
`LIVEKIT_SIP_HOST_PORT` and `LIVEKIT_RTP_HOST_PORT_RANGE`.

## When you do want a real call

Everything above runs without a carrier. Once a local call works, a real one is
the same command with one flag added:

```sh
unmute dev <your-package> --telephony --carrier
```

That flag brings a managed tunnel, points your number's webhook at this run, and
puts the webhook back the way it was when you stop. Two flags adjust it, and both
need `--carrier`: `--public-url` uses a tunnel of your own instead of the managed
one, and `--no-webhook` leaves your number's configuration untouched so you can
point it yourself. [Running a phone route locally](/dev/telephony) covers both.

It also says what its delay means:

```text
livekit: --carrier: this places a real call through your carrier.
livekit: audio delay in this mode is an artifact of reaching your machine from
livekit: the public network. It is not the delay a deployed agent has.
```

Going live still needs every step the run prints under `setup:`. A healthy local
call is not evidence about any of them, which is why those steps print on every
default run.
