The largest span in a voice turn is the model, and a tool turn costs two round
trips instead of one. So the cheapest turn is the one that never happens.

[`prefetch:`](/build/prefetch) is how you delete one. This page is about deciding
which ones are worth deleting, and it is written from doing it to
`examples/salon-concierge` and reading the traces afterwards.

<Note>
  The costs quoted here are measured, on two real calls of that agent. The
  savings are arithmetic: a removed round trip is a removed round trip. Measure
  your own agent before and after, because that is the only number that is about
  your agent.
</Note>

## Where this came from

Two traces, one per target, read span by span. Real `llm_request` p50 was
1.09s to 1.20s on both. Three things stood out, and all three were the model
spending round trips on facts that were already knowable:

| What the caller experienced | What it cost | What was already true |
|---|---|---|
| being asked for a phone number, digit by digit | 23.3s, 5 model round trips | the call arrived carrying the number |
| saying "tomorrow" and hearing nothing | 2.56s of silence, two chained tool hops | a clock had the date |
| being moved to a booking step | 2.07s of silence, two round trips | nothing needed discovering at all |

The third one is not a pre-fetch. It is a cover line, and it belongs to
[`announce:`](/optimization/overview#speak-before-a-tool-runs). Keeping the two
apart is most of the thinking here, so it is the last section on this page.

## Count round trips, not seconds

This is the habit worth building. When you look at a slow stretch of a call, do
not ask how many seconds it took. Ask how many times the model had to speak,
listen and speak again.

A round trip on our agents costs about 1.1s, and it is close to constant. So a
step's cost is roughly its round trip count times that number, plus whatever the
caller spends talking. The 23.3s step above was five round trips and a lot of
human speech, which is why removing round trips is the change that moves it and
tuning a connection is not.

The arithmetic then tells you what a pre-fetch is worth before you write it:

- a fact the model would have fetched with a tool: **two round trips**, because a tool turn is a call and a reply;
- a fact the model would have asked a person for: **at least two**, usually more, because people answer partially and get read back to;
- a fact already in the prompt: **zero**.

## Three kinds of fact

The test is one question. **Could this have been known before anybody spoke?**
Not "is it slow", and not "is it annoying to collect".

**A clock reading.** The cheapest win and the easiest to miss, because a date
tool looks like a legitimate tool. It is not: it takes no conversational input,
so it can only ever return something you could have put in the prompt. Ours was
called `get_current_date` and it is now deleted. Any tool whose arguments are
empty is this same case in disguise.

**A fact the transport carries.** A caller ID, a called number, a direction. The
agent is handed these before the greeting, and then in our case asked for one of
them out loud anyway.

**A lookup keyed on one of those.** This is where the real value sits, because
the answer is your own data. A number becomes a customer, an account, a booking
history. One read only tool call before the greeting replaces a conversation.

What is not on the list: anything the caller has to decide. Their appointment
time, the service they want, whether they are happy with a slot. No amount of
pre-fetching touches those, and a step that collects a decision is doing its job.

## Filling a value is enough. You do not rewrite the flow

This surprised us, and it is the part worth internalising.

The prerequisite guard already skips a step whose required values are present.
So a step that exists to collect something stops running the moment something
else supplies it. You add a `prefetch:` entry, and the step you wanted to avoid
gets skipped, with no edit to the step, the delegate, or the routing.

Which cuts both ways, so check it deliberately: a pre-fetch that fills a value
silently removes a step from the call. If that step also greeted the caller,
verified them, or set expectations, its removal is a behaviour change and not
just a speed one.

## Collecting becomes confirming

The 23.3s step is the headline, and the naive fix is wrong. A number from the
carrier tells you which handset dialled, not who is holding it. People ring from
a partner's phone or hold two accounts, and an agent that acts on caller ID alone
will eventually read one stranger's details to another.

So the step does not disappear. It changes job:

- **before:** ask for a number, hear part of it, read it back, get corrected, confirm. Five round trips.
- **after:** read back the number you already have, hear a yes. One.

That is the shape to aim for on any pre-fetched fact a person could dispute.
[`confirm:`](/build/prefetch#a-carrier-fact-is-a-proposal) enforces it: until the
caller agrees, the value satisfies no guard, appears in no prompt but the
confirming step's, and makes any tool using it refuse itself. You cannot forget
to confirm, because the compiler will not let the value be used.

<Tip>
  Confirmation is inherited. A name looked up from an unconfirmed number is
  itself unconfirmed. That is what stops a greeting from using it, and it means
  you mark the number rather than having to find every value derived from it.
</Tip>

## Do not move the wait to before the greeting

The trap. A pre-fetch runs before the agent says anything, so time spent there is
silence with nothing at all to listen to, not even a ringing tone's worth of
expectation. Dead air before hello is worse than dead air mid-call.

Three rules that keep it honest, and the first two are enforced:

1. The whole block has a **two second budget**. A lookup that overruns is logged and stepped over.
2. It **cannot fail a call**. A raise is logged and stepped over too.
3. Keep the number of entries small, and never chain a lookup off a lookup off a lookup. Entries run in order, so a chain spends the budget serially.

If your lookup is routinely slow, a pre-fetch is the wrong home for it. Leave it
as a tool the model calls mid-call with an `announce:` line over it, where the
caller has already heard a voice.

## Design for empty, because empty is the common case

Every source of a pre-fetched fact can be absent, and on some routes it always
is. The browser loop has no carrier. No Pipecat route supplies a caller number.
Callers withhold theirs. Your lookup will not know a first-time customer.

So the empty call is not the edge case to handle later. It is the case to write
first:

- Write each prompt so it reads as a whole sentence with the value empty. An unset variable renders as nothing, so "Your number is {{customer_phone}}." becomes "Your number is ." unless you write around it.
- Then check it still reads well once the value is there.
- Then run the agent both ways: `unmute dev` with `--source from_number=...` and without it. Both are real paths in production.

`unmute validate` tells you which entries can never resolve on a given target,
naming the route and the knock-on entries, so read its warnings rather than
discovering it on a call.

## A pre-fetch is not a cover line

Both make a gap shorter to sit through, and they are not interchangeable.

| | `prefetch:` | `announce:` |
|---|---|---|
| what it does | removes a round trip | gives the caller something to hear during one |
| when it runs | once, before the greeting | every time that tool fires |
| use it for | a fact that was already knowable | a fact that genuinely has to be fetched now |

The 2.07s gap on entering a booking step was neither a lookup nor a discovery, so
no pre-fetch could touch it. It got an `announce:` on the delegate instead.

Reach for a pre-fetch first, because a removed round trip is gone. Reach for an
announce for what is left. And keep the line shorter than the gap it covers: put
one on two tools that fire in the same turn and the caller hears two cover lines
for one request.

## Checking that you actually gained

One call proves nothing. We have seen two calls on an identical build differ by
1.1s of mean silence, which is a whole round trip of noise.

Make at least three calls per configuration, and compare **the span the change
touched** rather than the end to end number. A removed round trip is one of the
few changes clear enough to see in a single span comparison: count
`llm_request` spans inside the step you changed, and the count should have
dropped by a whole number.

The container also logs one line per entry, which is the fastest way to see
whether a pre-fetch resolved, skipped, or ran out of budget:

```text
prefetch today: resolved booking_date=2026-09-01
prefetch caller: skipped, the call carries no from_number
```
