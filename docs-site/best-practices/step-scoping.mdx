---
title: "Making a step actually run"
description: "A step the model can skip is a step that never writes state. What to give a step so it gets entered, and what to take away from the agent above it."
---

A step is worth having when it does something the agent above it cannot do as
well: a narrower prompt, a smaller context, a typed result that lands in
declared state. None of that happens if the model never enters it.

The model does not read your intent. It sees one list containing the agent's own
tools and the step's entry function, and it picks. These are the rules that
decide which way it picks.

## Give the step a tool the agent does not have

If the agent already holds every tool the step holds, the agent can finish the
job without entering the step. It usually will, and then the step's `assign:`
never runs and declared state never records what happened.

This is the failure it produces. A complaint specialist declared:

```yaml agent.yaml
complaint_specialist:
  tools:
    - look_up_refund_policy
    - record_complaint          # also the step's only tool
  tasks:
    - name: handle_complaint
      tools:
        - record_complaint
      assign:
        - complaints+: result.complaint
```

On a live call it recorded the complaint correctly, gave the caller a sensible
next step, and the state block still read `Complaints: none recorded yet` at the
end of the call. The specialist had the tool in reach, so it never entered the
step, so nothing appended to `complaints`.

The prompt was not the problem. Its workflow said "then run the complaint step
in the same turn, silently", in those words. A tool within reach beat the prompt.

The fix is to take the tool off the agent and leave it on the step:

```yaml agent.yaml
complaint_specialist:
  tools:
    - look_up_refund_policy
  tasks:
    - name: handle_complaint
      tools:
        - record_complaint
```

<Note>
`unmute validate` warns when an agent holds every tool of one of its steps, and
names the tool to move. It is a warning and not a refusal because whether the
model takes the short route depends on your prompt and your `when:`, neither of
which the compiler reads. Read the warning and decide.
</Note>

A step that declares no tools at all is fine. Its prompt, its context scope and
its typed result are reasons to enter it that have nothing to do with tools.

## Keep a read on the agent, keep the write on the step

The rule above has a natural shape once you apply it. Lookups that answer
questions belong on the agent, because the caller can ask at any time and
entering a step to answer a price question is slow. Anything that records,
saves, or changes something belongs on the step, alone, because that is the
action whose result you want in declared state.

Split that way, the model has a real reason to enter: the step holds the only
route to the thing the caller is asking for.

## Scope history down and let state carry the facts

Once a step reads declared state, it does not need the transcript, and sending
the transcript costs both money and accuracy.

| Scope | What the step sees | Use it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `full` | The whole conversation | The step needs the caller's exact wording, or you have not declared state yet |
| `messages` | What was said, without tool traffic | The step acts on what the caller asked for and records why |
| `reset` | Nothing but its own prompt and declared values | The step is fully described by what it requires |

`reset` is the strongest and the most demanding. A reset step never receives the
turn that triggered it, so it cannot know why the caller rang. One agent gave a
verification step a "reason" field to fill under `reset`, and the step asked the
caller what they were calling about on every call, immediately after they had
just said. Steps that act record why. A reset step records only what it is
handed.

## Give every step an escape, and read it

A step advertises its own tools plus its finish, and nothing else. When the
caller asks for something none of those cover, a step with no way out refuses,
the caller presses, and it refuses again.

Every generated step carries a reserved `unserved_request` field for this. The
step does its own work, then names the request it could not serve in its own
words, and finishes.

The half people forget is the other side. The agent that owns the step has to
act on that field. Say so once in the owning agent's prompt, and say it as a
job:

```md
A step handing back an unserved request has given you a job, not an excuse, so
act on it in the same turn and never apologise for it.
```

Left implicit, an agent will read the field, say something sympathetic, and end
the turn. On one call it apologised to the caller for a booking it was fully
able to make.

## Do not let two prompts disagree

A step's prompt and its owning agent's prompt are written at different times and
read together. When they conflict, the model picks one and you cannot predict
which.

The pairing that bites most often is "do not run the same flow again for a
request that just finished" on one side and "act on the unserved request" on the
other. A second booking is a new request, not a repeat of the last one, but
nothing says so unless you write it. Until it did, one call resolved the
contradiction by apologising to the caller instead of booking.

When you add a rule to a step, read the owning agent's prompt in the same
sitting and check it still agrees.
